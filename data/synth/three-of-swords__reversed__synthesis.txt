In the Three of Swords, the querent's mental faculties are turned inward, their faculties of reason and discernment muddled by the weight of their own emotional turmoil. This is not a state of balance, but rather a state of dissonance, where the usual harmonies of the psyche are disrupted by the presence of another, perhaps a person with whom the querent has compromised, or a part of themselves that has been repressed. The usual clarity of thought is replaced by confusion, as if the mind is wandering, lost in a spiritual alienation that is both a product of its own error and a source of its own suffering.
