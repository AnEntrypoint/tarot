In the Six of Wands, the triumphant figure stands poised on the precipice of expectation, their laurels freshly minted and their gaze cast outwards towards the horizon, yet beneath the surface of this seeming victory lies a complex web of power dynamics. The servant, the inferior, the one who toils in the background, may lose the confidence of their master, their own desires subsumed by the will of another. The crown of hope, that golden aura that surrounds the figure, is a double-edged sword, cutting both ways, as the weight of responsibility and the crushing burden of expectation threaten to topple the very edifice of triumph.
